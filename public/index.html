Holding Hands

Sea otters are widely known for their heartwarming habit of holding hands while they rest on the water's surface. This behavior, called rafting, prevents them from drifting apart on ocean currents. Mothers often wrap their pups in kelp fronds to anchor them safely while foraging for food below.

Groups of resting otters are called rafts, and these gatherings can include dozens or even hundreds of individuals. The sight of a large raft peacefully bobbing on the ocean is one of the most charming spectacles in marine wildlife. Otters communicate within the raft through soft vocalizations and gentle nudges.

Densest Fur on Earth

Sea otters hold a remarkable record: the densest fur of any animal on Earth, with up to one million hairs packed into a single square inch. Unlike whales, seals, and other marine mammals, otters have no layer of blubber beneath their skin to insulate against the frigid Pacific Ocean.

Instead, they depend entirely on their extraordinary coat to trap a layer of warm air next to their skin. Maintaining this insulation demands hours of meticulous grooming each day — rolling, rubbing, and blowing air into their fur to keep every strand clean and waterproof.

Tool-Using Mammals

Among the very few non-primate mammals known to use tools, sea otters stand out for their resourceful approach to feeding. They crack open hard-shelled clams, mussels, and sea urchins by placing them on their chest and striking them with a flat rock. Each otter often favors a particular rock, storing it in a pouch of loose skin beneath its foreleg.

This behavior is not instinctive — young otters learn it by closely observing their mothers. This makes sea otters a compelling example of cultural transmission, where knowledge is passed down through generations by observation rather than genetics.
